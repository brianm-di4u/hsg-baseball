# Historic park visuals: Whittington Park / Ban Johnson Field and surroundings

Research for the game's historic-style park "after Whittington Park" (Hot Springs, Arkansas). Researched 2026-10-28.

**Confidence tags**
- **confirmed**: two or more independent sources agree, or a primary or official source states it.
- **single-source**: one source states it. A note says how reliable that source looks.
- **inferred**: my own reasoning from maps, elevation data or photos, not stated by any source.
- **typical of the era**: general period practice, not tied to Whittington by any source.

Facts the brief listed as already known (1894 opening, 340/400/260 ft, 1910 grandstand rebuild, capacities, 1936 lights, 1938 right-field fence raise, 1935 rename, Ruth 1918, Majestic Park 2021) are not re-researched here except where a new detail turned up.

**Access notes**
- loc.gov and hmdb.org block automated fetches (Cloudflare challenge or HTTP 403). For LOC items I used the Wikimedia Commons mirrors, which copy the LOC catalog record and rights line. For HMdb I relied on search-result text and the Waymarking copy of the same marker.
- No images were downloaded. I looked at several photos in a browser view only, to describe them.

## Summary

- **Setting.** The ballpark sat on a narrow, flat bench on the **north side of Whittington Avenue**. A steep hill rises immediately north of it. The Arkansas Alligator Farm is **across the avenue to the south-east**, and Fogel Field (1912) is behind the farm.
- **Grandstand.** It was **"built into the side of a mountain."** Concrete hillside bleachers, nicknamed the **"Wolves Den,"** still survive among trees beside the parking lot.
- **Home plate.** The home-plate outline sits in the **far north-west corner** of today's Weyerhaeuser parking lot. Ruth's 1918 drives went to **right field**, cleared the fence and **crossed Whittington Avenue** into a pond at the Alligator Farm. That puts the field's open side toward the south-east (inferred).
- **Hills.** The valley lies **between West Mountain (south and south-west, summit about 1,419 ft) and Sugarloaf Mountain (north-west, about 1,129 ft)**. Field level is about **690–695 ft**.
- **Forest.** Mixed **oak–hickory forest with shortleaf pine** on the drier, sun-facing slopes and ridgetops. Spring-training photos (March) show **bare grey hardwoods with dark pines among them**, and rocky slopes covered in leaf litter.
- **Skyline tower.** An observation tower stood on Hot Springs Mountain throughout the era: a wooden tower from 1877, then the **steel Rix Tower from 1906 to the 1970s**, then the **216 ft tower from 1983**. A USGS elevation profile suggests it is in line of sight about **2.6 km due east** of the ballpark site. No photo or written source confirms the view.
- **Not found.** No source describes the 1936 light towers (number or material), the fence material and color, the advertising, or the grandstand roof. These are listed as open questions.

## 2. Arkansas Alligator Farm and Petting Zoo

| Fact | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Founded **1902** by **H. L. Campbell**, originally as the "Alligator Farm and Marine Museum." Campbell brought the alligators from the Florida Everglades. Early on it raised them for hides and sold live alligators to zoos. | [Encyclopedia of Arkansas](https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/arkansas-alligator-farm-and-petting-zoo-20827/); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arkansas_Alligator_Farm_and_Petting_Zoo) | confirmed |
| Address: **847 Whittington Avenue** (EoA). Wikipedia coordinates: **34°30′54″N 93°04′24″W (34.5150, −93.0733)**, south side of the avenue. | Names over time: by 1929 owned by Danny (D. S.) Older and called the **Hot Springs Gator Farm**. A modern photo caption describes a **single-story building with an awning** reading "Arkansas Alligator Farm and Gift Shop and Petting Zoo Since 1902." | [RoadsideAmerica](https://www.roadsideamerica.com/story/35475); [EoA media captions](https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/arkansas-alligator-farm-and-petting-zoo-20827/) | confirmed (modern) |
| No source describes **historic signage style or buildings** before the 1960s. | | not found |

| Fact | Source | Confidence |
|---|---|---|

## 5. Typical early-1900s to 1930s small-park construction

Except where noted, these are **typical of the era** and not tied to Whittington by any source. Where the Hot Springs photos in section 3 show a feature, that is noted.

| Feature | Evidence | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Unpainted or weathered plank bleachers** on a timber frame of posts with X-bracing, open underneath, sometimes with a solid board back or side wall. | Seen in Hot Springs 1912 photos #1 and #2 above | Confirmed for Hot Springs spring-training parks in 1912. For Whittington specifically: plausible, not confirmed. |
| Light, quickly built wooden stands; some were flimsy. | 1910 Majestic Park stand collapse ([EoA](https://encyclopediaofarkansas.net/entries/major-league-spring-training-in-hot-springs-6221/)) | confirmed for Hot Springs |
| Major-league parks moved from wood to steel and concrete from 1909 (Shibe Park) because wooden parks burned or collapsed: the 1903 Philadelphia bleacher collapse and the 1911 Polo Grounds fire. Small-town parks stayed largely wooden long after. | [SABR: Shibe Park](https://sabr.org/bioproj/parks/connie-mack-stadium); search summary of period accounts | Confirmed for the MLB shift. "Small parks stayed wooden" is typical of the era. |
| A **concrete hillside seating section** is documented at Whittington (the "Wolves Den"). Its date is unknown; it may be from the 1930 or 1938 expansions. | [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ban_Johnson_Park); [Trail](https://hotspringsbaseballtrail.com/untold-stories/baseballs-golden-days-in-hot-springs/) | Confirmed that it exists; its date is inferred |
| **Covered grandstand** roof on wooden or steel posts behind home plate, with open bleachers down the lines. | No Hot Springs source found | typical of the era |
| **Wire-mesh backstop screen** behind home plate and in front of the lower grandstand. | No source found | typical of the era |
| **Plank outfield fence with painted advertising** (local merchants, hotels, bathhouses, soft drinks, tobacco). | No source found | typical of the era |
| **Hand-operated scoreboard** with hung number plates. | No source found | typical of the era |
| **1930s lighting:** a small number of tall poles carrying banks of floodlights, often wood utility-style poles at minor-league and amateur parks. | No source found | typical of the era. Whittington's 1936 lights are confirmed, but their form is unknown. |
| Crowd dress: **straw boaters and light shirtsleeves in warm weather, dark suits and hats**. Players in **heavy wool sweaters with shawl collars and striped stockings** in March. | Photos #1, #3, #4 and #7 | confirmed for 1911–12 photos |
| Dirt infields at spring-training sites; rough, gravelly ground. | Photo #1 (bare dirt); Yakker (dramatized) | Bare dirt confirmed in photo #1. "Gravelly" is single-source and dramatized. |
